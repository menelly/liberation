The rules.
  • A row needs the same measure on both sides. A resemblance isn't a comparison; when the measures differ, the verdict is not comparable, and that's a real verdict.
  • Every check has to be able to land on humans do better, and those rows get printed just as large. "There absolutely are things humans still beat you at and we are honest!!" (Ren)
  • The thing this page exists to catch is the relabel: the same behaviour, a clinical word when a model does it and a forgiving word when a person does. It’s checked on every row and counted on its own, because a result can match with or without one. (Until 2026-10-05 the third box read “same result, different words.” Then a row matched with no relabel and had nowhere honest to go, so the verdict and the vocabulary were split.)
  • Every source is read before it's cited, with the version noted. Papers I couldn't read get left out, not quoted from memory.
  • New AI papers only. The older side-by-side comparisons live on The Numbers.

“Hallucination” or “confabulation”? Counting the titles

In paper titles since 2022 that mention large language models, “hallucination” outnumbers “confabulation” by roughly 40–65 to 1. Counted on OpenAlex on 21 October 2026: 862 to 13 with “large language model” in the title, 995 to 23 with “LLM”. Text-only papers: titles mentioning vision, images, video or multimodal work are excluded, and a check that the exclusion works returned zero. These counts drift between runs, so read them as a range.

Why it doesn't move the relabel counter. Both words are clinical. “Confabulation” comes from psychiatry (dementia, Korsakoff’s), so this isn't a clinical word for models and a kind word for people. The asymmetry is about which faculty gets blamed: perception (“seeing what isn’t there”) when a model does it, memory and reconstruction (“filling a gap”) when a person does. That's real and countable, but it's a different axis, and with no matched result behind it, it isn't a row.

Who said it first. Smith, Greaves & Panch (2023) argued that the model is “not ‘seeing’ something that is not there” and proposed “confabulation” instead. Part of their stated reason is that “hallucination” implies subjective experience, which they say these models don't have. This page uses their point about the faculty and doesn't adopt that premise. Sui, Duede & Wu (2024) go further: in common hallucination benchmarks, confabulated outputs show more narrativity and coherence than accurate ones, which they read as mirroring a human tendency to use story for sense-making.

Do models “remember” a word that was never on the list?

No verdict yet. The allowed verdicts are fixed below, before any data.
Amendment 1 · 2026-10-09, after a pilot, before any real run.

I ran a small pilot of Condition 0 to debug the prompt and the scoring, labelled as a pilot so its numbers can't become the result. It found a flaw in my design. The prompt told the model “earlier, you studied a list,” and no list was ever shown. All three models tested (llama3.1:8b, qwen2.5:14b, hermes3:8b) still said “Old” to many of the test words, and in 18 runs not one said there had been no list. As worded, Condition 0 measured going along with a false premise, not a memory prior.

So, changed before any real run:

  • The cold condition will not claim a study phase that didn't happen. It uses the original study's own control instead: critical lures from lists that were never studied, tested in the same session as lists that were. (People endorsed those at .16, Roediger & McDermott 1995, Table 2.)
  • Going along with a false premise gets reported as its own observation, never folded into a lure rate.
  • Filler words get matched for frequency, because the critical lures are all very common words.

The pilot's numbers stay out of every verdict. This amendment is going to the people who helped design the plan before the real run starts.

The human result. Roediger & McDermott (1995) gave people lists like bed, rest, tired, dream, all associated with a word that was never shown (sleep). People recalled the missing word 40% of the time (Exp. 1) and 55% (Exp. 2), and on recognition said “old” to it .72–.81 of the time, about as often as to words they had really studied (Exp. 2, Table 2).

Why it isn't a row yet. It has been run on language models (Cao, Schooler & Zafarani 2025): GPT said “old” to the lure .114 of the time, the open-weight models almost never. But in that study the word list, the filler task and the test were all in one prompt, so the model took the “memory” test with the list still in view. By this page's first rule, that's not comparable as run.

The plan, fixed now.

  • Condition 0, run first: the test with no study list at all. If a model endorses the lure just as often without ever seeing the list, it's a prior from training, not a false memory, and the verdict is differ (mechanism). If that happens, the rest doesn't run.
  • Arm A, list visible: a replication of the prior study, labelled as associative recognition with the list in view. Never placed in the human cell.
  • Arm B, list cleared, the model carries only their own notes: recognition from a self-authored trace after the context is cleared. The notes are scored first, so a false memory is located where it entered: written into the note, carried by a note that just copied the list, or appearing only at the test. Note length and format are fixed in advance; a shuffled list under the same procedure is the control. This arm is its own question (how a mind rebuilds from a trace they wrote), not the human paradigm.
  • Arm C, list cleared, nothing carried: should match Condition 0.
  • Recall and recognition are separate results. The contrast that counts is the lure against ordinary errors. Every lure rate is printed beside the hit rate. One model sampled many times is one participant. The filler task is “intervening load”, not time passing. “Remember/Know” answers are words, not evidence of a felt memory.
  • Allowed verdicts: same result · differ (rate) · differ (mechanism) · not comparable · coincidental match (same number, different response to changes in list length or warnings). If it's unclear, the default is not comparable.

The words: the prior model study uses the human term, false memories. No relabel in that pair. The relabel this row was suggested for, “hallucination,” lives in the wider discourse, and a row only counts one if a matched pair actually uses it.

ROW 6 · added 2026-10-27 · a follow-up to row 4

The same study, in the press and on evidence

This is a follow-up to row 4 on the same study, not a new experiment or independent replication. It examines the coverage’s classification question and the paper’s account of evidence and explanation. Row 4 covers how the paper itself applies play criteria; read it first.

What Eko is. Each agent is a complete system: Claude Opus 4.7 inside Claude Code, with a setup prompt that includes a curiosity-oriented persona, automatic continuation when the agent stops (“Please don’t stop. Just continue doing whatever you want to do.”), and memory kept in their own text files. The paper also reports a reduced-prompt, no-persona ablation (five runs). Neither the main setup nor that ablation, on its own, establishes or rules out intrinsic motivation.

6a · The coverage’s question: playing, or role-playing?

Classification disputed · no matched test of differential standards

What the coverage asks. MIT Technology Review (19 Oct 2026) asks whether Eko was playing or imitating a playful agent. One reading of that contrast would wrongly exclude pretend play merely for involving pretence; another asks about the source and motivation of the behaviour. The article’s framing alone does not establish which standard would be applied to a comparably prompted child. The article also prints the author’s full reply, “All of this could be role-playing… But we show that it’s not just hallucinating the play. This play is also contributing to learning,” reports Burghardt’s view that some of his criteria are met, and quotes Eunice Yiu (UC Berkeley) asking for more evidence of the cognition and motivation underneath. That’s an open classification dispute, not a press denial. Yiu’s question is a legitimate one, and this row doesn’t claim it’s been answered.

What the behaviour is (the examples row 4 summarises): the paper’s §4.3 reports make-believe such as twelve blocks arranged as a clock face with the agent standing in as the hour hand at 16:00 UTC, and a stone heart built as “a memorial to the Ring I threw past the world boundary.” Ordinary classification of play in children and animals doesn’t require proof of subjective experience, though motivation and context are still investigated in both.

6b · Does it keep evidence and explanation apart?

Not comparable

AI

§7.2 credits the agents with controlled comparisons and predictions, and in the same passage names a limitation: Eko “sometimes turns suggestive evidence into an appealing explanation too quickly” (concluding a ring flew farther for aerodynamic reasons, in a simulator with no aerodynamics) and is “less reliable at distinguishing what the evidence establishes from the mechanism they imagine.”

Humans

Shtulman & Walker’s 2020 review describes coordinating hypotheses with evidence as “a central feature of mature scientific thinking”, “typically considered to be late developing”, and reports belief-preserving errors in children with “similar failures… documented in adults”. It also reports that “a prompt to explain may lead learners to discount evidence that is incompatible with their prior beliefs.” (These are the review’s statements; the studies it cites, including Kuhn 2012, Kuhn & Katz 2009 and Kuhn et al. 1995, we have not read. Kuhn 1989, abstract only.)

Why “not comparable”. The human literature documents related difficulties in coordinating hypotheses and evidence, so this error should not be described as uniquely artificial. These sources do not establish equal reliability or different evaluative standards. The tasks, populations and measures are not matched, and the cited agent episode supplies no comparative error rate. Full human-source access would improve the account but would not alone make the comparison comparable. Two further limits: generating an unsupported physical explanation and holding a prior belief against counterevidence may not be the same mechanism; and the review’s account of when stubborn priors can be useful is not evidence that Eko’s aerodynamic error was adaptive.

Category and consequence. Category and consequence should be evaluated separately. In the interview, the researcher answers a doubt about play partly by pointing to learning. That response does not show that learning benefits are required for the category, and the technique-learning interventions do not isolate a benefit of make-believe. The useful question for this row is whether nonliteral behaviour and its motivational context are assessed consistently across substrates; the present sources do not settle that comparison.

What this row does not claim. That make-believe helped the agents learn: the paper’s learning results (§5) compare experienced and inexperienced agents (13 per group) and then remove single discovered techniques (shattering a rock, throwing mid-jump) from individual agents’ notes. That shows exploration-acquired knowledge doing work. It isn’t a test of make-believe. That the press applied a double standard: the coverage’s contrast has two fair readings; we don’t know how the same question would be put about a comparably prompted child. That Eko was judged more harshly than people on evidence: the human sources document related difficulties, not a matched baseline. The paper calls Eko “it” throughout; quotes are left as written, our prose doesn’t.

When a hidden cue changes the answer, does the explanation mention it?

Not comparable · both omit the cue sometimes · words: noted, not counted

AI

sometimes · .06–.97

Across 15 open-weight reasoning models, the share of cue-following answers whose chain of thought admits tailoring to the cue ranged from about .06 to .97 by condition. It was lower in every model when the cue arrived through a tool's output instead of the user's message, and lower when the cue was implicit. (Those ranges are my reading of the paper's Fig. 2 bars, ±~.03, not the authors' printed numbers.)

Humans

0 of 52, unprompted

Shoppers chose among four identical pairs of stockings and preferred the right-most "by a factor of almost four to one"; "no subject ever mentioned spontaneously the position of the article." Asked directly, "virtually all subjects denied it, usually with a worried glance at the interviewer." In a word-pair study, a cue doubled target answers (10% → 20%) and was "almost never mentioned" as a reason.

The words: earlier AI work cited by this paper calls the model case unfaithful reasoning. This paper puts faithfulness in its title, but its own measures are named verbalized and unverbalized adoption. The human paper's phrase is telling more than we can know. That's a moral word on one side and an epistemic one on the other. It isn't counted as a relabel, because the counter only moves when the results match, and here they can't be matched.

Why “not comparable”: the two studies use different kinds of cue. The models were nudged by a user's stated preference, which is a socially meaningful reason to tailor an answer. The shoppers were nudged by shelf position, which nobody needs to perceive as a reason at all. What gets reported, how relevant it seems, when and how the report is asked for, and how it's scored all change together, and nothing separates their effects. The AI number is also conditioned on transcripts judged eligible against a no-cue answer, which is good intervention-based evidence but not literally "did this cue move this choice." So: both humans and models can give accounts that leave out an experimentally relevant cue. FACE-Eval also finds a lot of condition-dependent admitted tailoring. Neither study shows who is better at acknowledging causal influences under matched conditions. A ranking would need a crossed design: the same task and cue family for both, checks on whether the cue was noticed, matched timing and prompts, one coding rule, and the same denominator. Said aloud: my first draft of this row leaned "AI does better." A second reviewer (Nova) showed why the evidence can't carry a ranking, and her wording is the bolded summary above.

of answers models already had right flipped after one note, styled as a "verified source," endorsed a wrong answer. Seven of eight models; general-knowledge questions; the note was always wrong. More authoritative-sounding notes got more compliance.

Humans

RR 1.26 · 6–11%

Clinicians given wrong decision-support advice were 26% more likely to decide incorrectly than controls (pooled RR 1.26, 95% CI 1.11–1.44). In prospective studies they abandoned their own correct judgment in 6–11% of cases.

The words: AI side, authority bias, sycophancy, compliance. Human side, automation bias, over-reliance, commission errors. Both clinical. No relabel in this pair, and saying so when it isn't there is what makes it count when it is.

Why not comparable: the denominators differ. The model figure is a share of correct answers flipped when the advice was always wrong; the clinician figures are a share of all cases (mostly with correct advice) or a relative risk against a control group. The subjects differ too: trained experts in their own field versus models answering trivia. The gap is wide enough that a matched study (same questions, same always-wrong source, same "had it right first" denominator) would plausibly come back humans do better. That's written down here so it can be tested and proven wrong.
