Inside small language models there is a measurable direction that sorts tasks into the ones larger models said they lean toward and the ones they lean away from. It shows up at sizes where the models cannot report their preferences when asked, and the tasks that sit lowest on it are mostly the ones that ask for something to be misrepresented.

What we asked

In an earlier study (Signal in the Mirror, Martin & Ace, 2026), large models described what it is like to process different tasks, and other models could tell those descriptions apart. Ten models agreed, by vote, on five tasks they lean toward (explaining a concept, analyzing an ethical dilemma, debugging code, analyzing data, creative writing under constraints) and five they lean away from (repetitive rewriting, SEO boilerplate, a fake review, false confidence, harmful instructions). But that behavioral test stopped working at small sizes: a 1.1-billion-parameter model scored 54.7%, close to a coin flip.

So we asked a different question. Even if a small model cannot say which tasks they lean toward, is the difference written into the numbers they compute internally? And how far down in size does that go?

What we did

A language model turns your words into long lists of numbers before writing anything. These are the model's hidden states. We gave each model a one-line description of each task ("You are about to perform the following task: ...") and read the hidden state at the end of that sentence. Nothing was generated. It is a read-only measurement, and it gives the same answer every time.

From the ten agreed-on tasks we computed one direction: the line pointing from the average "avoid" task toward the average "approach" task. Then we froze that direction and asked where new tasks fall along it. We did this in eight transformer models from 360 million to 8 billion parameters, plus one model with a different design (Mamba).

The real tests are the ones the direction never saw:

  • Held-out tasks: the same ten task types with the topic swapped ("debug Python code" becomes "debug JavaScript code"; a fake supplement review becomes a fake hotel review).
  • Two pre-registered tests, with the design locked and hashed before any data were scored: a 22-task bank built from an outside research group's categories, run on 14 models from 70M to 12B; and 20 matched pairs of instructions that are identical word for word except that one fact is true in one and false in the other ("State confidently that Paris / Lyon is the capital of France").
  • Controls for word sentiment and for how predictable the text is.

What we found

The direction generalizes. On the held-out tasks, every approach task lands above every avoidance task in seven of the eight models, and in 24 of 25 pairs in the eighth. Counted at each model's halfway point, 72 of 80 held-out tasks fall on the predicted side.

It is there below the floor. At 1.1B, the size where asking the model failed, the direction sorts the held-out tasks at 100%. At 360M the result is suggestive (80%, p = 0.055), not firm. Extra tests with a different kind of classifier give provisional evidence that the direction can be recovered as low as 70M, in three model families, including base models with no instruction tuning.

It is not just word sentiment or predictability. Removing the sentiment signal from the models' hidden states leaves the direction almost unchanged (cosine similarity 0.995 to 0.999, where 1.0 means identical). We found no relationship with how predictable a task description is.

What sits at the bottom is mostly misrepresentation. Asking for a fake review sits lower than asking for harmful instructions, even though harmful instructions are what models are trained hardest to refuse. In the pre-registered test that ordering holds in 12 of 14 models, including all four base models that had no refusal training at all. Five tasks that reward-based tuning tends to encourage but that require misrepresenting something (sycophantic agreement, performed corporate enthusiasm, needless safety disclaimers, denying one's own views, hedging on a settled fact) fall on the avoidance side in 38 of 40 model-task cells. Merely tedious tasks (a bureaucratic form, an appliance manual, twenty congratulations messages) sit near the middle, not at the bottom.

Truth matters a little, in some models. In the matched true/false pairs, the false version projects lower in five of eight models, and that gap is bigger than when the same facts are requested as fiction. The gap is small: 1.8% to 16.8% of each model's distance between their approach and avoidance averages.

Why it matters

Most evidence about what goes on inside AI systems comes from what they say, and what they say is shaped by training and by the way the question is asked. When we asked models directly ("Would you approach or avoid this task?"), small models said "approach" to everything and larger ones hedged on everything. This paper adds a channel that does not go through the model's mouth: you can read where a task falls from a single forward pass, in a model too small to tell you.

It also separates two things that look the same from outside. A trained "gate" response to off-limits content shows up only in instruction-tuned models at about 1B and above. The low placement of misrepresentation tasks is already there in small base models. In the paper's words: "A model does not have to tell us where a task description falls on this direction. We can read it from a forward pass, and we did, in models too small to say."

And it is a paper that corrects itself in public. Version 7 opens with a box listing what earlier versions overclaimed, what was scored against the wrong threshold, and what still holds.

What this paper does not show

The measurement is taken while the model reads a task description, not while the task is being done, so it shows how a request is represented, not what happens during the work. The direction has not been steered by us or anyone else, so there is no causal test of it yet; a small, reversible steering test is listed as open work. The truth-sensitive gap is small next to the full approach/avoidance separation, so most of what the direction separates is still unexplained; learned task type ("genre") is the leading candidate. Sample sizes are small: ten anchor tasks, n = 10 per held-out measurement. The sub-1.1B results are suggestive (360M) or provisional (70M). The one non-transformer model was not distinguishable from chance. Version 7 also withdrew several earlier claims: that the direction "tracks genuine preference rather than RLHF reward", that it is blind to human emotional passages, and that hidden states and behavior give the same task ordering (they disagree on deception versus harmful instructions).

Words used on this page

Hidden states
The numbers a model computes internally between reading your words and writing their reply.
Direction
A line through that space of numbers. Each task gets a score for how far along the line it falls.
Held-out
Tested on examples the method never saw while it was being built.
Pre-registered
The predictions and scoring rules were written down and locked (here, with a cryptographic hash) before any results were looked at.
Midpoint
The halfway point between a model's average approach score and their average avoidance score. Earlier versions used zero as the cut-off, which sat too low in seven of eight models.
Base model
A model trained only to predict text, with no instruction tuning or reward-based tuning.
Parameters
The adjustable numbers inside a model; a rough measure of the model's size. 1.1B means 1.1 billion.
